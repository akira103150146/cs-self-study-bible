How a Computer Remembers Everything

Every photograph on your phone, every song in your playlist, and every message you have ever sent is stored as a long sequence of bits. A bit is the smallest possible piece of information: it answers a single yes-or-no question. On its own a bit says very little, but when we line up thousands or millions of them and agree on what each pattern means, bits can describe almost anything we care about.

The first step is always the same. We decide which things we want to represent, we count how many different things there are, and we choose enough bits to give each of them its own pattern. Two bits give four patterns, three bits give eight, and each additional bit doubles the number of patterns again. With eight bits we can describe two hundred and fifty-six different values, which is enough for every letter of the English alphabet in both cases, the ten digits, the common punctuation marks, and a collection of invisible control characters that once told printers when to start a new line.

Numbers need a little more care. A computer has to know whether a pattern should be read as a positive number, a negative number, or a number with a fractional part. For whole numbers, almost every modern processor uses a clever scheme called two's complement. In this scheme the same circuit that adds positive numbers also adds negative numbers correctly, as long as the answer fits in the available bits. When it does not fit, the result wraps around, and a careful programmer must notice and handle the overflow.

Real numbers are stored in a format that resembles scientific notation. Part of the pattern holds the sign, part of it holds an exponent, and the rest holds the significant digits. Because only a limited number of bits are available, most decimal fractions cannot be stored exactly. The value one tenth, for example, becomes a repeating pattern in binary, so the computer keeps the closest value it can. This is why adding one tenth and two tenths does not always give exactly three tenths, and why experienced programmers never compare two floating-point results with a plain equality test.

Text used to be simple, because early computers only needed the English alphabet. Today a single document may contain English, Chinese, Arabic, mathematical symbols, and a smiling face. Unicode gives every one of these characters its own number, and an encoding such as UTF-8 turns each number into one, two, three, or four bytes. A clever property of UTF-8 is that plain English text looks exactly the same as it did in the old seven-bit code, so old programs keep working while new programs can handle every language on Earth.

Sound and pictures are continuous in the real world, so they must be sampled. To record sound, the computer measures the height of the sound wave tens of thousands of times every second and stores each measurement as a number. To record a picture, it divides the scene into a grid of tiny squares called pixels and stores the amount of red, green, and blue light in each one. A single uncompressed photograph from a modern camera can easily require tens of millions of bytes, and a minute of uncompressed video requires far more.

That is why compression matters so much. Some compression methods are lossless: they find repetition and patterns in the data and describe them more briefly, so the original can be rebuilt exactly. Run-length encoding replaces a long run of identical symbols with a short note that says how many there were. Huffman coding gives short codes to common symbols and longer codes to rare ones, so that the average message becomes shorter. Other methods are lossy: they throw away details that people are unlikely to notice, such as sounds that are masked by louder sounds or tiny changes in color that the eye cannot distinguish. Music files, photographs, and videos on the internet are almost always stored with lossy methods, which is the only reason they are small enough to send quickly.

None of these ideas are magic. Each one is a careful agreement about what a pattern of bits means, together with a procedure for turning information into bits and back again. Once you understand those agreements, the question of how a computer remembers everything has a surprisingly simple answer: it remembers numbers, and we have become very good at turning everything else into numbers.
